Loss of opportunity can arise in commercial disputes where a party alleges that another party's conduct prevented it from pursuing a business transaction, securing a contract, completing a project or achieving a financial outcome. Assessing such claims requires careful consideration of the available evidence, the commercial circumstances and the uncertainty surrounding what might otherwise have happened.

For solicitors and other legal professionals, the central challenge is distinguishing a genuine, evidence-supported commercial opportunity from a speculative expectation. A forensic accountant can assist by examining the financial records, testing the assumptions used to quantify the alleged loss and explaining the financial implications of different possible outcomes.

This article examines how forensic accountants may analyse loss of opportunity claims in commercial disputes, the evidence that can support the assessment and the issues that require particular care.

## What Is Loss of Opportunity in a Commercial Dispute?

Loss of opportunity generally refers to an alleged financial loss arising because a party was deprived of the chance to pursue or benefit from a commercial opportunity.

Examples may include:

- A business alleging that it lost the opportunity to secure a contract following an alleged breach of agreement.
- A company claiming that another party's conduct prevented it from completing a proposed transaction.
- A business arguing that it lost the chance to expand into a new market because of an alleged contractual failure.
- A party alleging that it could not pursue a commercial project because required services, assets or information were not provided as agreed.

The precise legal test and the extent to which a claim may be recoverable depend on the applicable law, the contractual terms, the evidence and the circumstances of the dispute. A financial expert does not determine legal liability or decide whether the legal requirements for recovery have been met. Those are matters for the relevant legal process.

The forensic accountant's role is to examine the financial aspects of the alleged opportunity and assess whether the proposed valuation is supported by the available evidence and an appropriate analytical approach.

## How Loss of Opportunity Differs from Loss of Profits

Loss of opportunity and loss of profits may overlap, but they are not necessarily identical.

A loss-of-profits claim may focus on profits allegedly lost from an established business activity, contract or trading arrangement. A loss-of-opportunity claim may involve a commercial prospect that had not yet resulted in a completed transaction or an established stream of income.

For example, a company may allege that it was prevented from bidding for a substantial contract. The financial analysis would need to distinguish between the value of the opportunity to compete for the contract and the profit that might have been earned if the contract had been awarded and performed.

The existence of an opportunity does not, by itself, establish that the expected revenue or profit would have been achieved. The assessment may need to consider whether the opportunity was sufficiently developed, the likelihood of success, the expected costs of performance and the commercial risks involved.

Where a claim involves profits allegedly lost from an identifiable business activity, the principles discussed in the [Loss of Profits and Quantum service](/services/loss-of-profits-quantum) might also be relevant.

## The Role of a Forensic Accountant in Loss of Opportunity Claims

A forensic accountant can help legal teams evaluate the financial evidence underlying a loss-of-opportunity claim. The work will depend on the dispute, the questions posed to the expert and the information available.

### 1. Establishing the Commercial Opportunity

The first step is to understand the opportunity that the claimant says was lost.

Relevant questions may include:

- What transaction, contract, project or commercial activity was being pursued?
- How advanced were the negotiations or preparations?
- Were key commercial terms agreed or still under discussion?
- Were financing, regulatory approval, customer approval or other conditions outstanding?
- What evidence demonstrates the claimant's ability and intention to proceed?
- What circumstances might have affected the outcome independently of the alleged conduct?

Contemporaneous documents can be particularly important. These may include draft agreements, correspondence, tender submissions, management accounts, budgets, board papers, customer communications and internal forecasts.

The expert should distinguish between documented commercial developments and assumptions made after the dispute arose. A proposal, expression of interest or preliminary discussion may demonstrate that an opportunity existed, but it may not establish the likely financial outcome.

### 2. Reviewing the Financial Evidence

The expert may examine financial records to understand the expected commercial performance of the proposed opportunity.

Depending on the circumstances, this may involve reviewing:

- Historical financial statements and management accounts.
- Sales forecasts, budgets and business plans.
- Contract proposals, tender documents and pricing schedules.
- Customer orders, pipeline records and correspondence.
- Cost estimates, staffing requirements and operational capacity.
- Financing arrangements and working-capital requirements.
- Comparable transactions or relevant market information.

The purpose is to establish whether the financial assumptions are consistent with the contemporaneous evidence and the claimant's commercial position at the relevant time.

Forecasts should not automatically be treated as reliable indicators of future performance. Their preparation, underlying assumptions, track record and consistency with other evidence may all require examination.

### 3. Assessing the Likelihood of Success

An opportunity may have had commercial value even if success was not certain. However, uncertainty about the outcome can materially affect how the alleged loss should be analysed.

For example, a business that submitted a tender may have faced competition from several qualified bidders. The analysis would need to consider the evidence concerning the tender process, the claimant's pricing and capabilities, the competing bids where available, and any conditions that remained unresolved.

Other opportunities may depend on securing funding, obtaining approvals, negotiating final contractual terms or demonstrating that the claimant had sufficient operational capacity.

A forensic accountant can assess the financial implications of these uncertainties and test alternative assumptions. The expert should not assign numerical probabilities simply because a calculation requires them. Any probability-based approach should have a clear rationale and be supported by appropriate evidence, where such an approach is suitable to the issues in dispute.

The assessment of legal recoverability and the legal significance of a lost chance remain matters for the court, tribunal or other relevant decision-maker.

## Methods Used to Assess the Financial Value of an Opportunity

There is no single method suitable for every loss-of-opportunity claim. The approach depends on the nature of the opportunity, the evidence available and the financial question being examined.

### Expected Financial Outcome

Where the evidence supports an analysis based on different possible outcomes, the expert may examine the financial consequences associated with each scenario.

For example, a proposed project might have depended on winning a contract, completing the work within a particular period and achieving a forecast margin. The expert could analyse the revenue, costs and resulting profit under the relevant scenarios, taking account of the assumptions and uncertainties involved.

Any weighting applied to alternative outcomes must be justified. A calculation that multiplies an estimated profit by an unsupported probability may give an appearance of precision without a reliable evidential basis.

### Comparison with the Position Without the Alleged Conduct

A financial assessment may require comparison between the claimant's actual position and a hypothetical position in which the alleged wrongful conduct did not occur.

This involves identifying what would reasonably have happened in the alternative scenario, based on the available evidence.

The analysis may need to account for:

- The timing of the proposed transaction or project.
- The revenue that could realistically have been generated.
- The costs that would have been incurred.
- Operational and financial constraints.
- Market conditions and competitor activity.
- Alternative opportunities available to the claimant.
- Other events that may have affected the outcome.

The hypothetical scenario should be grounded in the facts and should not assume that every commercial objective would necessarily have been achieved.

### Sensitivity and Scenario Analysis

Where the result depends on uncertain assumptions, sensitivity analysis can help show how the estimated financial outcome changes when key inputs vary.

Relevant variables may include expected sales volumes, contract duration, profit margins, implementation costs, completion dates and the likelihood of satisfying contractual conditions.

Scenario analysis can also help distinguish between a more favourable outcome, a less favourable outcome and a central case based on the available evidence.

These techniques do not eliminate uncertainty. Their value lies in making the assumptions visible and demonstrating how strongly the result depends on them.

## Common Challenges in Loss of Opportunity Claims

### Incomplete or Contemporaneous Evidence Gaps

A claim may be difficult to quantify where the parties did not finalise the commercial terms or where records of negotiations and forecasts are incomplete.

The expert may need to reconstruct the commercial position using available correspondence, financial records, transaction documents and other evidence. Any limitations should be identified clearly rather than concealed through unsupported assumptions.

### Overly Optimistic Forecasts

Business plans and projections may reflect management expectations rather than outcomes that were reasonably achievable.

An expert should examine the basis for forecasts, compare them with historical performance where relevant and consider whether the assumptions were realistic in the circumstances at the time.

Later events may provide relevant information in some cases, but they should not automatically be used to treat an uncertain opportunity as though its success had been assured.

### Failure to Account for Costs

Revenue is not the same as profit. A business may have needed to incur additional labour, materials, financing, distribution, marketing or other costs to pursue the opportunity.

A sound financial assessment should consider the costs that would have been incurred and avoid presenting gross revenue as though it represented the financial loss.

The treatment of fixed costs, incremental costs, avoided expenditure and any other relevant financial effects will depend on the facts and the methodology adopted.

### Overlap with Other Heads of Loss

Commercial claims may include several alleged losses arising from the same events. These can sometimes overlap.

For example, a claim may include lost profits from a proposed contract alongside a separate amount for the loss of the opportunity to secure that contract. Depending on the basis of the claims, adding both amounts without examining their relationship could result in double counting.

The expert should identify the financial basis of each claimed amount and explain whether the calculations overlap. The legal treatment of the heads of loss is a separate question for the legal team and the relevant decision-maker.

### Unclear Distinction Between Fact and Assumption

An assessment may depend on a combination of documented facts, management estimates and hypothetical scenarios.

These categories should be distinguished clearly. Where an assumption cannot be verified, the expert should explain its significance and, where appropriate, assess the effect of alternative assumptions on the calculation.

## Instructing a Forensic Accountant on a Loss of Opportunity Claim

Clear instructions help define the financial issues and ensure that the expert's work addresses the questions arising in the dispute.

Solicitors may wish to provide:

- A concise summary of the dispute and the alleged commercial opportunity.
- Relevant contracts, draft agreements and correspondence.
- Financial statements, management accounts and forecasts.
- Tender documents, transaction records and supporting commercial evidence.
- Details of the losses claimed and the calculations already prepared.
- Relevant procedural requirements and the intended use of the expert's work.
- Specific questions concerning the financial assumptions, methodology and evidence.

The expert should be given sufficient context to understand the disputed issues while maintaining appropriate independence. Instructions should not require the expert to adopt a predetermined conclusion.

For further practical guidance, see [What to Include in Instructions to a Forensic Expert](/insights/instructions-to-forensic-expert).

The expert's report should explain the material evidence reviewed, the methodology used, the assumptions adopted, the limitations of the analysis and the reasons for the conclusions reached. Where the evidence does not support a reliable estimate, that limitation should be stated.

## How Financial Analysis Can Assist Commercial Dispute Resolution

A carefully structured financial analysis can help the parties and their legal advisers understand the financial basis of a loss-of-opportunity claim.

It may identify which assumptions are supported by evidence, where additional information is required and how different scenarios affect the proposed valuation. It can also clarify the distinction between an identifiable commercial opportunity and a financial outcome that remains uncertain.

The value of the analysis depends on the quality of the evidence, the suitability of the methodology and the transparency with which uncertainty is addressed. A financial expert can assist with these matters but does not replace legal analysis of liability, causation or recoverability.

## Conclusion

Loss-of-opportunity claims require more than a forecast of the profits a business hoped to earn. The assessment must examine the commercial opportunity, the evidence that it could have progressed, the financial consequences of the relevant scenarios and the uncertainties that affect the outcome.

A forensic accountant can assist by testing financial assumptions, analysing alternative outcomes, accounting for relevant costs and identifying potential overlap between claimed losses. A clear explanation of the evidence, methodology and limitations helps legal professionals evaluate the financial issues in a commercial dispute.

The appropriate approach will depend on the facts of the case, the applicable legal framework and the questions the expert has been instructed to address.
